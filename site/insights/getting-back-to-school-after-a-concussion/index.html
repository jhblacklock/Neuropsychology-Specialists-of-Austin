Getting back to school after a concussion

Return to learn usually comes before return to play, and it is the part schools are least set up for.

The instructions you left the clinic with were mostly about sport. Rest, then a graded return to activity, then clearance to play. Somewhere in there was a line about school.

School is the harder problem, and it usually comes first. A child is back in a classroom long before they are back on a field, and a classroom is cognitively demanding in exactly the ways a recovering brain finds difficult.

Recovery is directed by your child's physician. This post is about the school side of it.

Why a school day is hard after a concussion

Sustained attention, working memory, reading fluency, and the tolerance for noise and light are the functions most often affected after a concussion, and a school day makes continuous demands on all four. A child who feels fine at home for two hours can be overwhelmed by period three.

Symptoms that get worse as the day goes on are the usual pattern, which is why "he was fine this morning" is not evidence that a child is ready for full days.

Return to learn comes before return to play, and it is the part schools are least set up for.

What a graded return looks like

Published guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and from the American Academy of Pediatrics describes return to school as a staged process rather than a date. The stages below are the shape of it; your physician sets the pace.

Stage What it looks like Moving on
Rest, briefly Home. Light cognitive activity as tolerated rather than a dark room. Symptoms settle with light activity
Light activity at home Short bouts of reading or schoolwork, stopped before symptoms build. Can sustain 30 minutes
Partial days Half days, core subjects, scheduled breaks, no tests. Gets through a half day without symptoms escalating
Full days with support Full timetable, reduced workload, extra time, breaks available. Symptoms no longer provoked by a full day
Full return Supports withdrawn one at a time, missed work made up on a plan. —

Accommodations that actually help

  • Reduce the work, do not defer it. A pile of make-up assignments waiting at full return is the most common way a good recovery comes apart.
  • Extra time, and permission to stop. A child who can leave a room before symptoms peak recovers faster than one who pushes through.
  • No high-stakes testing during recovery. A score taken mid-recovery misrepresents the child and then sits in the file.
  • Someone named. One person at the school who tracks the plan. Without that, accommodations survive about two weeks.

When neuropsychological testing is worth doing

Most concussions resolve without any need for formal testing, and testing every concussed child would be an expensive way to learn nothing. It becomes useful when the picture stops being straightforward:

  • Symptoms persist beyond the recovery window your physician expected.
  • Academic performance has not returned even though the child reports feeling well.
  • There is a pre-existing condition — ADHD, a learning disability, anxiety — and nobody can separate what is new from what was already there.
  • A school needs documentation to formalise accommodations that have been running informally.

In those cases testing gives you a measurement rather than an impression, and a baseline for the next one.
