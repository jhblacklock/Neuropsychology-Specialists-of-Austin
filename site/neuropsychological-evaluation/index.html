Neuropsychological Evaluation

An evaluation measures how your child's brain is actually working.

It is a diagnostic testing procedure. It uses behavioral tests rather than scans, and it is sensitive to how specific brain areas and circuits are functioning.

What it tells you that a scan does not

A neurological exam, an EEG, and brain imaging describe structure and electrical activity. None of them tells you whether a child can hold two instructions at once, or recall what they read twenty minutes ago.

Testing measures those things directly. It gives information those other procedures do not, and it puts numbers on cognitive strengths, weaknesses, and symptoms so they can be compared — to other children of the same age, and to your child at a later date.

What is measured

Seven domains, chosen to fit the question

  • Motor function
  • Memory, visual and verbal
  • Sensory-perceptual function
  • Language
  • Executive functioning
  • Attention
  • Intellectual functioning

A psychoeducational component — reading, arithmetic, written expression — is added where it will support school planning. For most school-age referrals, it is.

An evaluation also assesses the effect of a neurological or medical problem on emotional adjustment and quality of life. A child who cannot keep up is usually aware of it.

Which specific areas are tested depends on the concern, your child's age, and current functioning. That is decided at the consultation, not in advance.

Which children should be evaluated

  • Children at risk for cognitive problems or developmental delays, where strengths and weaknesses need identifying rather than guessing.
  • Children who are not progressing in school, and the school data does not explain why.
  • Children showing attention or behavior problems.
  • Children with a known neurological or medical condition, where the effect on learning has to be measured before anyone plans around it.

The practice sees children and adolescents. Cognitive and executive coaching extends to young adults. Confirm

Conditions we address

  • ADHD
  • Learning disabilities
  • Dyslexia, dyscalculia, dysgraphia
  • Autism spectrum disorder
  • Traumatic brain injury and concussion
  • Epilepsy
  • Brain tumors, and the late effects of chemotherapy and radiation
  • Genetic and chromosomal disorders
  • Cerebral palsy and spina bifida
  • Effects of prematurity
  • Executive functioning difficulties
  • Other neurodevelopmental and neurocognitive disorders

What you do with the results

  • Identifies strengths and weaknesses, which aids diagnosis and drives specific treatment recommendations.
  • Predicts which treatments, services, compensatory strategies, or classroom accommodations will help.
  • Tracks development or cognitive function over time, or after treatment.
  • Anticipates which situations will be difficult later — the next grade, the next transition — so they can be planned for.

Family education is part of the feedback session. Where psychotherapy, cognitive rehabilitation, or another intervention is warranted, we refer — or, where it is one of ours, we provide it.
