Neuropsychological Evaluation

An evaluation measures how the brain is actually working.

It is a diagnostic testing procedure. It uses behavioral tests rather than scans, and it is sensitive to how specific brain areas and circuits are functioning.

What it tells you that a scan does not

A neurological exam, an EEG, and brain imaging describe structure and electrical activity. None of them tells you whether you can hold two instructions at once, or recall a list an hour later.

Testing measures those things directly. It gives information those other procedures do not, and it puts numbers on cognitive strengths, weaknesses, and symptoms so they can be compared — to other people your age, and to you at a later date.

What is measured

Seven domains, chosen to fit the question

  • Motor function
  • Memory, visual and verbal
  • Sensory-perceptual function
  • Language
  • Executive functioning
  • Attention
  • Intellectual functioning

An evaluation also assesses the effect of a neurological or medical problem on emotional adjustment and quality of life.

For students, a psychoeducational component — reading, arithmetic, written expression — can be added to support school planning.

Which specific areas are tested depends on the concern, the person's age, and current functioning. That is decided at the consultation, not in advance.

Who should be evaluated

Adults

  • Mental abilities have changed and a diagnosis needs establishing.
  • A condition is already known, and its effect has to be measured before treatment or rehabilitation is planned.
  • Return to work or to running a household is being considered after an injury.
  • Family members need to understand the condition and how to manage it.
  • The diagnosis is unclear and a sensitive measure of symptoms would settle it.

Children

  • A child is at risk for cognitive problems or developmental delays and their strengths and weaknesses need identifying.
  • A child is not progressing in school.
  • A child is showing attention or behavior problems.

Conditions we address

  • Traumatic brain injury and concussion
  • Stroke
  • Brain tumors
  • Epilepsy
  • Alzheimer's disease
  • Parkinson's disease
  • Multiple sclerosis
  • Dementia
  • ADHD
  • Learning disabilities
  • Dyslexia
  • Autism spectrum disorder
  • Other neurocognitive disorders

What you do with the results

  • Identifies strengths and weaknesses, which aids diagnosis and drives specific treatment recommendations.
  • Predicts which treatments, services, compensatory strategies, or accommodations will help.
  • Tracks development or cognitive function over time, or after treatment.
  • Anticipates which situations will be difficult later, so they can be planned for.

Patient and family education is part of the feedback session. Where psychotherapy, cognitive rehabilitation, or another intervention is warranted, we refer — or, where it is one of ours, we provide it.
